## My Perspective on Credit Risk

Credit risk management requires a continuous balance between portfolio risk and lending volume. Tighter approval policies can reduce exposure, but they also exclude borrowers who might have repaid successfully. The practical challenge is to identify a relatively small segment with disproportionately high risk, so that intervention is targeted and the broader business can continue to grow within its risk appetite.

This is where effective risk variables matter. I look for signals that help explain where risk is concentrated and that can support a concrete decision. A useful variable must be assessed through several lenses: its ability to distinguish risk, its coverage, its timeliness, its stability, and the business cost of acting on it. Strong separation in a historical sample is only one part of that assessment.

### Predictive Strength and the Lag in Model-Based Variables

Model-based variables can combine multiple signals and capture relationships that are difficult to express through simple rules. Their limitation is the time needed for repayment outcomes to mature. Training depends on periods with observable risk performance, so a model necessarily learns from an earlier lending environment. Its predictive strength in that environment does not guarantee that the same relationships will hold after conditions change.

My work on multiple-lender borrowing illustrates this tension. From October 2025, as new lending-assistance rules took effect, I observed institutions leaving the market and a marked shift in the underlying external inquiry data. Changes in the participants represented in a data source can alter the meaning of a variable, even when its calculation remains unchanged. The variables derived from the model showed strong risk differentiation, but their interpretation also had to account for this shift in the external environment.

For me, this makes monitoring the underlying data just as important as evaluating the final score. Changes in coverage, missingness, institution mix, and variable distributions can signal that a historical relationship needs to be reassessed. A change in data availability should not automatically be interpreted as a change in borrower behavior.

### Behavioral Signals for Earlier Intervention

Variables defined around observable behavior offer another way to respond. A tag for borrowing to repay existing loans describes a pattern with an interpretable risk mechanism: repayment capacity may depend on continued access to new credit. Such a tag can help identify exposure to an emerging liquidity shock before a new set of repayment outcomes has fully matured. Its usefulness still depends on a careful definition and on distinguishing ordinary refinancing from sustained dependence on new borrowing.

In a June 2026 case I worked on, the failure of a lending-assistance platform was followed by losses for funding partners and tighter funding conditions. We judged that customers with pronounced reliance on new loans to service existing debt could become more vulnerable as external credit availability contracted. Identifying this segment supported an early policy response, including targeted rejection before the potential deterioration appeared fully in repayment data.

### Bringing the Signals Together

Model-based signals and behavioral tags serve complementary purposes. Models can capture complex historical patterns; interpretable tags can help translate an emerging risk mechanism into an actionable customer segment. Neither is inherently sufficient. A tag can be too broad, while a model can become less reliable as its data environment changes.

My approach is to connect each variable to a clear risk hypothesis, test the population it identifies, and consider the consequences of the proposed action. The aim is to preserve useful lending capacity while addressing concentrated risk. This requires balancing predictive strength with freshness, interpretability with coverage, and risk reduction with the volume lost through intervention.
